EIL313 Listening Practice · Unit 1 · Graph discussion

Learning Our Heritage: Elders, Videos, and Classes

Three classmates discuss a chart for a sociology assignment about how young adults learn about their family heritage.

Name: ______________________   Date: ____________   Score: ____ / 12

1. Before you listen: key words

traditional adjectivefollowing customs that have existed for a long time
generation nounall the people born at about the same time
adapt (to) verbto change something so it fits a new situation
heritage nountraditions, art and history passed down from the past
trend nouna general direction in which something is changing
decade nouna period of ten years
access (to) nounthe chance or right to use something
global adjectiveabout or including the whole world
analysis nouna careful study of information to understand it
diversity nouna mix of many different types of people or things

2. While you listen: notes

Listen for signposts (first, but, the main reason is, so to sum up). Write key words, not sentences.

Trend 1 (numbers)
Trend 2 (numbers)
Explanations they give
Do they agree? Final idea

3. Questions

1. What is the conversation mainly about?

  1. Why young people changed the way they learn heritage
  2. Whether young people are losing their heritage
  3. Whether family elders still matter to young people
  4. Which way of learning heritage works the best

2. After "No, wait. Look again," what does Mei point out on the graph?

  1. In twenty seventeen, online is above the elders
  2. In twenty thirteen, the elders are above online
  3. In two thousand nine, classes are above online
  4. In twenty twenty-one, the elders are above classes

3. Mei says, "It's like they switched places." Which part of the graph does she mean?

  1. Online and elders where they cross, at about forty-five
  2. Online and classes where they cross, at about twenty
  3. Online at the end and elders at the start, both at seventy
  4. Classes and elders at the end, only ten points apart

4. After Josh describes all three lines, what is Mei's main point?

  1. The elders line fell, and videos can't teach the old language
  2. The elders line fell, and online grew faster than any other line
  3. The elders line fell, and young people stopped asking about it
  4. The elders line fell, and classes aren't taking the elders' place

5. What is Josh's main point when he talks about his cousins?

  1. Videos teach them songs, but not what the songs mean
  2. Videos teach them songs, but not the dances that go with them
  3. Videos teach them songs, but not their grandpa's language
  4. Videos teach them songs better than their grandpa can

6. Why does Sofia ask the others to add up the numbers for twenty twenty-five?

  1. To question the idea that one source has replaced another
  2. To show that Josh read some of the numbers wrong earlier
  3. To show that the survey made a mistake with its numbers
  4. To show that online is as big as the other two together

7. What does Sofia's grandma probably want?

  1. For Sofia to sing the song back to her
  2. For Sofia's brother to learn the song too
  3. For Sofia to sing the song at a family party
  4. For Sofia to post the song online for relatives

8. How does Josh respond when Sofia questions his idea about access?

  1. He keeps it in the analysis as a possible reason
  2. He keeps it in the analysis and promises to find a source
  3. He leaves it out of the analysis and admits it was a guess
  4. He leaves it out of the analysis and still believes it

9. Why did Mei's cousin join a class?

  1. So a teacher could check the moves of her hands
  2. So she could meet people from other cultures
  3. So a teacher could check the steps of her feet
  4. So she could learn the part the videos skipped

10. Near the end, what does Mei tell Sofia?

  1. It was Sofia's grandma's song that changed her mind
  2. It was her own cousin's dance that changed her mind
  3. It was Josh's cousins and their songs that changed her mind
  4. It was Sofia's point about forty percent that changed her mind

11. Josh says he is convinced, "Mostly." What does he probably still worry about?

  1. That the elders line will keep falling after twenty twenty-five
  2. That the classes are still too small to take the elders' place
  3. That he doesn't know the old songs as well as his cousins do
  4. That he can't talk easily with his grandpa in his language

12. What does Sofia's last line show?

  1. She teases Mei about her sad speech earlier
  2. She teases Josh about the facts from his uncle
  3. She teases Josh about the call to his grandpa
  4. She teases Mei about her cousin's dance step

4. After you listen: discuss

  1. Who taught you the most about your family heritage: elders, the internet, classes, or something else?
  2. Sofia's grandma sends her songs in voice messages. Do you know other ways technology helps families share traditions?
  3. Josh's reason for the online jump came from his uncle. When you write about a chart, how can you show the difference between a fact and a possible reason?

Transcript

Josh: Okay, so for our sociology assignment, we have to write an analysis of this chart.

Mei: Yeah. It shows how young adults learn about their family heritage. Survey data from one Pacific city, two thousand five to twenty twenty-five.

Sofia: And it's people from eighteen to twenty-five. So, basically, us.

Josh: Okay, there are three lines. First, family elders. You know, grandparents, aunties, uncles.

Mei: Mm-hm.

Josh: In two thousand five, seventy percent of young people said they learned a lot from their elders. Then it starts to drop. By twenty seventeen, it's only forty-two.

Sofia: Wait, from seventy down to forty-two?

Josh: Yeah, a big fall. But look, after that it levels off. It's forty in twenty twenty-one, and still forty in twenty twenty-five.

Mei: Okay, so the decline stops at forty.

Josh: The second line is online videos and social media. This one's the opposite. It starts low, at ten percent.

Mei: Only ten?

Josh: Yeah, ten. It goes up a little, to fifteen in two thousand nine. And then it jumps, to thirty-five in twenty thirteen. So by twenty thirteen, it's already passed the elders.

Mei: No, wait. Look again. In twenty thirteen, the elders are still at fifty. Thirty-five is lower.

Josh: Oh, you're right. Sorry. It passes them later, before twenty seventeen. Online is fifty-five there. By twenty twenty-five, it's seventy percent.

Sofia: Wow. So it climbs really sharply.

Mei: Seventy? Oh! That's the same number the elders had at the start. It's like they switched places.

Josh: And the third line is cultural classes and clubs. It stays flat at twenty percent for more than a decade.

Mei: And then?

Josh: Then it rises gradually. Twenty-five in twenty twenty-one, and thirty in twenty twenty-five.

Mei: So what does it all mean? Honestly, I think it's sad. The traditional way was learning from your elders. And the classes aren't taking their place. They're still only at thirty. In other words, my generation is losing its heritage.

Josh: I agree with Mei. For example, my cousins know all the old songs from videos. But they can't really talk with our grandpa in his language.

Sofia: Hmm. Can I show you something? Look at twenty twenty-five. Forty, seventy, thirty. Add them up.

Josh: Uh, that's a hundred and forty. Wait, that's more than a hundred. So the survey is wrong?

Sofia: No, it's not wrong. The reason is, people could choose more than one source. I'd choose two myself.

Mei: Two? Why?

Sofia: Well, every Sunday my grandma sends me a voice message on my phone. She sings one of the old harvest songs. At the end, she always asks, 'Is your little brother listening too?' So is that elders, or is that online?

Mei: Huh. I guess... both?

Sofia: Exactly. So it's not one or the other.

Mei: Okay, but still. Elders went from seventy to forty. That's a real drop.

Sofia: Sure, it dropped. But forty percent is still a lot.

Josh: Here's my idea about the online jump. The main reason is access. Around twenty ten, the city put free wifi in all the public libraries.

Sofia: Maybe. But where did you hear that? It's not on the chart. Is it a fact, or just a possible reason?

Josh: Ha. Okay, my uncle told me. So I'll leave it out of our analysis. But I still think he's right.

Mei: And what about the classes? Why do they go up at the end?

Josh: Maybe it's because of global culture. Online, you see so much diversity, cultures from all over the world. So you want to learn your own.

Mei: Maybe. But for my cousin, it was simpler. She learned a dance from videos and got the hands right. But she kept doing one step wrong. So she joined a class, so a real teacher could check her feet.

Sofia: Oh, that's interesting. So the videos sent her to the class.

Mei: Yeah. Then she called our grandma to ask what the dance means. Wait. That's three sources for one dance.

Josh: Video, class, and grandma. Ha.

Mei: Okay. I think I changed my mind. And Sofia, it wasn't my cousin's dance. It was your grandma's Sunday song. I don't think my generation is losing its heritage. We're adapting. We mix old and new.

Sofia: I'll take it!

Josh: Okay, I'm convinced too. Mostly. But I owe my grandpa a phone call this weekend. And I'd better practice some of his words first.

Mei: So, what do we write?

Josh: So, to sum up. The trend for elders went down from seventy, then leveled off at forty. Online went up sharply, from ten to seventy. And classes stayed flat, then rose a little, to thirty.

Mei: And the main point is, young people don't learn from just one source anymore. They mix them.

Josh: I'll start writing. Sofia, can you check the numbers?

Sofia: Sure. Josh, this time, no facts from your uncle!
