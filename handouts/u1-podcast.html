Do Traditions Have to Stay the Same?

On the Campus Minds podcast, the host Maya interviews Dr. Helen Ward, an anthropologist, about whether traditions have to stay the same.

Name: ______________________   Date: ____________   Score: ____ / 12

1. Before you listen: key words

traditional adjectivefollowing customs that have existed for a long time
generation nounall the people born at about the same time
adapt (to) verbto change something so it fits a new situation
heritage nountraditions, art and history passed down from the past
perceive verbto notice and understand something in a particular way
interpret verbto decide what something means
conform (to) verbto behave in the way that most people in a group expect
principle nouna basic idea or rule that explains how something works
mentor noun / verban experienced person who advises and helps a less experienced person; to do this
acknowledge verbto accept or admit that something is true

4. After you listen: discuss

  1. Think of a tradition in your family or culture. How has it changed from your grandparents' generation to yours?
  2. Dr. Ward says change isn't the same as loss. Do you agree? Can you think of a change that went too far?
  3. Who taught you a tradition? Would you like to be a mentor and teach it to someone younger?

Transcript

Maya: Hello everyone, and welcome to Campus Minds. I'm Maya. Today's question matters to me: do traditions have to stay the same to survive? I'm here with Dr. Helen Ward. Welcome!

Dr. Helen Ward: Thanks, Maya. So, I'm an anthropologist. That's a long word, but it just means I study people: how they live and the customs they share. I've spent about fifteen years talking with families about their traditions.

Maya: Fifteen years? Wow.

Dr. Helen Ward: Yes, fifteen. And I'm still learning!

Maya: Okay, I'll be honest. I'm a bit worried about this topic. My grandmother's generation knows all the old ways. My generation... well, we're mostly on our phones. Sometimes I feel like our heritage is just, um, slowly disappearing.

Dr. Helen Ward: Mm, I hear that a lot. But try seeing it another way. Here's my main point. Traditions don't survive by staying frozen. They survive because each generation adapts them. In other words, change isn't the same as loss.

Maya: Change isn't loss. Hmm. Funny, that's almost word for word what my grandmother says! But what does it look like in real life?

Dr. Helen Ward: Well, anthropologists talk about cultural transmission. Basically, that's how customs pass from older people to younger people. Let me give you an example. In many Pacific Island families, woven mats are important wedding gifts. The traditional way is that the women in the family weave them.

Maya: Right, I've seen those. They're beautiful.

Dr. Helen Ward: They are. Well, I met a young woman who wanted to weave a mat for her cousin's wedding, but she didn't know how. So her aunt became her mentor. Every Saturday, her aunt taught her, step by step. Her mat had some new colors. But the principle was the same. The gift still said, "We will be there when you need us."

Maya: Oh, I like that. So the colors changed, but the meaning didn't.

Dr. Helen Ward: Exactly. Now, Maya, what about your family? Is there a New Year dish?

Maya: Yes! My grandmother's always asking me to learn her New Year soup. And I always say, "Next year, Grandma. I'm too busy."

Dr. Helen Ward: Ha! Grandmothers hear that a lot. Well, food is a great example. Many Vietnamese families make a special square rice cake for the New Year. Traditionally, it's wrapped in a certain green leaf. But families who move abroad often can't find that leaf, so many of them use banana leaves instead.

Maya: Mm-hm. Banana leaves.

Dr. Helen Ward: Now, some people perceive that as a loss. They say, "That's not the real thing." But the families I talked to interpret it differently. You might think the important part is making the cakes together. But for them, it's the square shape. The square stands for the earth.

Maya: So it depends on how you look at it.

Dr. Helen Ward: Precisely. Same cake, two different ways of seeing it.

Maya: Hmm. And what about greetings?

Dr. Helen Ward: Yes! Here's one more example. In many Korean families, young people bow to their elders at the New Year. But today, grandparents often live in another country. So the grandchildren bow to a camera, on a video call.

Maya: Wait, really? They bow to the screen?

Dr. Helen Ward: They do! And the grandparents I met loved it. Now, I'll acknowledge it's not the same. The grandparents can't hand the children their New Year money. But the respect is still there.

Maya: Huh. Okay, you're changing my mind... a little.

Dr. Helen Ward: Only a little?

Maya: Well... I still have a question. Some people say a tradition only works if everyone does it exactly the same way.

Dr. Helen Ward: Right. That's the idea that people must conform. In other words, everyone follows the group's rules exactly. Now, there's some value in that. Fixed rules make it easy for newcomers to learn. However, when a tradition is too strict, people often just change it quietly, at home. So, if we want a tradition to last, it needs some room to change.

Maya: But then who decides how much change is okay? At what point is it a totally different thing?

Dr. Helen Ward: Good question, and there's no easy answer. Some say the elders. Others say the whole community, together. My answer? The people who actually do the work. If you cook the New Year meal every year, your voice counts more. The key thing is to keep asking, "What does this tradition mean to us?"

Maya: Hmm. The people who do the work... In my family, that's my mom! She does all the cooking now. Grandma just gives orders! But I'll admit, I feel more hopeful than at the start.

Dr. Helen Ward: That's all I can ask for.

Maya: So, to sum up: Dr. Ward's view is that traditions last because each generation adapts them. Change doesn't have to mean loss. Thanks so much, Dr. Ward.

Dr. Helen Ward: Thank you, Maya. And, um, maybe this is the year for that soup?

Maya: Ha! Okay, okay. No more "next year." Thanks for listening! See you next time on Campus Minds.
