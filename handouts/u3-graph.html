Riverton: Rent vs. Income

Three classmates in an urban studies class prepare a presentation about a chart of rent and income in a made-up city.

Name: ______________________   Date: ____________   Score: ____ / 12

1. Before you listen: key words

impact nouna strong effect
policy nounan official plan or set of rules made by a government or organization
density nounhow many people or things are in an area
infrastructure nounthe basic systems a city needs: roads, pipes, drains, power
estimate verb / nounto calculate roughly; a rough calculation
median nounthe middle value in a list of numbers
substantially adverbby a large amount
trajectory nounthe direction or path of development over time
demand nounhow much people want to buy something
emission noungas or pollution sent into the air

2. While you listen: notes

Listen for signposts (first, but, the main reason is, so to sum up). Write key words, not sentences.

Trend 1 (numbers)
Trend 2 (numbers)
Explanations they give
Do they agree? Final idea

3. Questions

1. In the summary at the end, which part comes second?

  1. The causes: the train and a city rule
  2. The impact: what the rent rise means for people
  3. The numbers: how rent and income changed
  4. The air: how extra driving adds pollution

2. After Sofia mentions the professor's thirty percent rule, which year's numbers does she give?

  1. Twenty thirteen
  2. Twenty sixteen
  3. Twenty nineteen
  4. Twenty twenty-two

3. Josh says, "the worst part is over," and Mei disagrees. How does Josh answer her?

  1. He points out that rent rose only a hundred dollars in three years
  2. He points out that income kept rising after twenty twenty-two
  3. He points out that the big jump lasted only three years
  4. He points out that the share of income stayed at half

4. Mei talks about the median income. What is her main point?

  1. Students earn much less than the median, so rent takes more of their pay
  2. Students often share rooms, so they pay less than the median rent
  3. Students move often, so they always pay the newest, highest rents
  4. Students live far out, so they pay for gas as well as rent

5. Use the graph. When was the train line announced, and when did it open?

  1. Announced at the start of the jump, opened at the end
  2. Announced at the start of the jump, opened a year later
  3. Announced a year before the jump, opened at the end
  4. Announced at the start of the jump, opened after rent leveled off

6. Mei says, "That's kind of surprising." What surprises her?

  1. Rent went up even in towns that didn't get a station
  2. Rent went up before the train was even running
  3. Rent went up even though income was going up too
  4. Rent went up after the parking rule had ended

7. What was the city's policy, according to Sofia?

  1. Every new home had to be near a train stop
  2. Every new home could have only two floors
  3. Every new home needed two parking spaces
  4. Every new building could hold only two families

8. What is Sofia's main point about the causes?

  1. The train mattered more, since rent jumped while it was being built
  2. The train mattered more, since people moved in as soon as it was announced
  3. The housing rule mattered more, since the reading ranks it first
  4. The housing rule mattered more, since other towns on the line saw little rise

9. How does Josh finally respond to Sofia's view of the causes?

  1. He accepts her view after Mei and Sofia explain density
  2. He accepts her view because she did the reading
  3. He accepts her view after she mentions the other towns
  4. He accepts her view once she agrees the train started it

10. Josh says high rent is "an air problem too." Why?

  1. People who can't pay rent near the center live farther out and drive
  2. Cheaper homes are far from shops, so people drive to buy food
  3. People drive to the new train station, so the roads get busier
  4. Families who pay high rent can't afford newer, cleaner cars

11. Josh says the trajectory "looks better now." How does Sofia answer?

  1. She says maybe, but income is growing too slowly
  2. She says the line only looks flat for now
  3. She agrees, because incomes will soon catch up
  4. She agrees, because the city changed a rule

12. What will Sofia probably do next?

  1. Make the slides that show the big rent jump
  2. Ask the professor whether thirty percent is still fair
  3. Find out if other cities dropped rules like Riverton's
  4. Find out why rent rose less in the other train towns

4. After you listen: discuss

  1. In your home city or town, is rent easy or hard for young people and families to pay? What do people do when rent is too high?
  2. Would you rather pay more to live close to campus or work, or pay less and travel farther every day? Why?
  3. Should a city build more apartment buildings when many people want to live there? What could be good or bad about that?

Transcript

Mei: Okay, our presentation is on Monday, and we have to explain this chart. Josh, can you start?

Josh: Sure. The chart shows median monthly rent and median monthly income, from twenty ten to twenty twenty-five.

Sofia: Wait, sorry. What does median mean, exactly? Is it the same as the average?

Josh: Not quite. For example, put everyone's rent in a line, low to high. The one in the middle is the median.

Sofia: Oh, got it.

Josh: Okay, first, rent. In twenty ten, it was nine hundred dollars a month. Then it rises steadily. One thousand, eleven hundred, and twelve hundred in twenty nineteen.

Mei: Mm-hm. Not so bad.

Josh: No, but then look. Between twenty nineteen and twenty twenty-two, it jumps sharply, to nineteen hundred dollars.

Mei: Nineteen hundred? From twelve hundred?

Josh: Yeah. Seven hundred more in three years. After that, it levels off. Two thousand in twenty twenty-five.

Sofia: Okay. And the other line is income?

Josh: Right. Income climbs slowly the whole time, from three thousand dollars a month to four thousand.

Mei: So income went up a thousand. That's good, right?

Josh: True, but rent more than doubled. Income only went up by a third. So rent rose substantially faster.

Mei: Okay, but what does that mean for real people? Can we estimate how much income goes to rent?

Josh: Sure. In twenty ten, it's nine hundred out of three thousand. That's thirty percent.

Mei: Thirty. Okay.

Josh: And in twenty twenty-five, it's two thousand out of four thousand. In other words, fifty percent. Half!

Mei: Half? Wow. So families pay half their money for a home.

Sofia: Our professor says thirty percent of income or less is okay. But in twenty nineteen, it was twelve hundred out of thirty-six hundred. Already a third.

Josh: But the good news is, it's leveling off. It only went from nineteen hundred to two thousand. So I think the worst part is over.

Mei: Hmm, I'm not sure I agree. It stopped, but it stopped really high. In twenty twenty-two, it was nineteen hundred out of thirty-eight hundred. Already fifty percent.

Josh: Right, half. And in twenty twenty-five? Also half. That's my point. It didn't get any worse.

Mei: Huh. Okay, fair. But think about the impact on students. Most students earn way less than the median income. My cousin works two jobs, and rent still takes most of her pay.

Sofia: That's my part, the causes. The reading gave two reasons.

Josh: Let me guess. More people moved there?

Sofia: Yeah, that's the first one. In twenty nineteen, the city announced a new train line to the big city. It only opened in twenty twenty-two. But people moved in right away. Demand went way up.

Mei: Wait, so the infrastructure wasn't even finished, and rent already went up? That's kind of surprising.

Sofia: I know, right? But the second reason is the bigger one, I think. The city had a policy that every new home needed two parking spaces.

Josh: Parking? How does that matter? The jump happened while the train was coming. Isn't the train the main cause?

Sofia: The train started it, sure. But two other towns on the line got stations, and their rent rose only a little. In Riverton, because of the parking, builders couldn't fit apartment buildings. So density stayed low.

Mei: Wait, density is, like, how many people live in one area?

Sofia: Mm-hm. Low density means few people in lots of space. So, not enough homes.

Josh: Okay, okay. So it's both. The train brought people, and the policy left no room for them.

Sofia: Both, yes. But the parking rule is bigger. That's what I'll say.

Josh: Fine. You did the reading, not me. I'll go with your version.

Mei: Oh, and if people can't pay rent near the center, they move past the last train stop. Then they have to drive.

Sofia: Right. More driving, more traffic, more emissions. You know, pollution.

Josh: Huh. So high rent isn't just a money problem. It's an air problem too.

Mei: Ha, nice. Put that on a slide. Okay, so to sum up...

Josh: Let me try. First, rent rose much faster than income. Nine hundred to two thousand dollars. Thirty percent of income to fifty.

Mei: Second, the impact on students and families. Half their money goes to rent, and students earn even less than the median.

Sofia: And finally, the causes. The train raised demand, but the parking rule kept the number of homes low.

Josh: But the trajectory looks better now, right? The line's flat. Maybe it won't get any worse.

Sofia: I actually think so too. The reading says the city dropped the parking rule in twenty twenty-three. Builders are starting apartments now.

Mei: Oh, nice, a solution! Did other cities do the same thing?

Sofia: Leave that with me. I've got the reading list. Josh, can you do the graph slides?

Josh: Sure. I'll make that big jump easy to see.
