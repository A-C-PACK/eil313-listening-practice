Is Memory Like a Video Camera?

  4. Vivid memories can be wrong; most forgetting comes early; mix topics when you practice.

2. What do Maya's mom and brother say happened at the river?

  1. Maya fell into the water, and her brother pulled her out.
  2. Maya's older brother slipped and fell into the water.
  3. Their mother fell in while she was carrying the food.
  4. Maya and her brother both fell into the cold water.

3. Why does Dr. Hale probably call Maya's story "a classic"?

  1. Many people remember frightening moments more clearly than calm ones.
  2. Many people's early memories come partly from old family photos.
  3. Many people's early memories come partly from stories the family retells.
  4. Many people see their childhood memories from the outside, like a viewer.

4. Maya says her memory is "so vivid." How does Dr. Hale answer her?

  1. He describes a study of students who were sure about wrong memories.
  2. He tells her about a mistake in one of his own childhood memories.
  3. He asks her a small question about the picnic to test her memory.
  4. He jokes that his own family argues about stories like this one.

5. What does Dr. Hale compare consolidation to?

  1. Writing something in pen, not pencil
  2. Putting a book back on the right shelf
  3. Pressing save on a document
  4. Pouring concrete and letting it dry

6. What is Dr. Hale's main point about sleep?

  1. You need a full night's sleep before new material is saved.
  2. Even a short nap after class can help you keep new material.
  3. Sleeping well the night before a test helps you keep new material.
  4. Going to bed at one set time helps you keep new material.

7. Dr. Hale jokes about students who study all night. How does Maya react?

  1. She says she was planning to do it before her exam on Friday.
  2. She says she did it last week and jokes that it worked.
  3. She says she did it once and promises never to do it again.
  4. She says her roommate does it and jokes that it works for her.

8. Why does Dr. Hale call one part of his best strategy "the surprising part"?

  1. Most people expect testing themselves right away to work best.
  2. Most people expect to forget slowly over the first few weeks.
  3. Most people expect sleep to have nothing to do with studying.
  4. Most people expect a vivid memory to be a correct one.

9. In Dr. Hale's example of the best strategy he knows, what do you do first?

  1. Cover your notes and say the answers out loud
  2. Answer the practice questions in your textbook
  3. Make flashcards and quiz yourself with them
  4. Close your notebook and write what you remember

10. What is Dr. Hale's main point about rereading?

  1. Reread your notes in a new place, not always at your desk.
  2. Reread your notes after a self-test, not before it.
  3. Reread your notes out loud, not silently in your head.
  4. Reread your notes over several days, not all in one night.

Transcript

Maya: Welcome to Campus Minds. I'm Maya. Is memory like a video camera? My guest today studies exactly that. Dr. Robert Hale, welcome!

Dr. Robert Hale: Thanks, Maya. I study everyday memory and, um, how students can learn better.

Maya: So, the big question. When I remember something, it feels like I'm pressing play on a video. Is that what's happening?

Dr. Robert Hale: That's what most people think. But no. In my field we say memory is a reconstruction. What I mean by that is, your brain doesn't keep a full recording. It keeps pieces. Every time you remember, it builds the event again from those pieces.

Maya: Wait, it builds it again? Every time?

Dr. Robert Hale: Every time. In other words, remembering is more like… telling a story again. And when you tell a story again, you can alter it a little without noticing.

Maya: Hmm. I actually have a story like that. When I was seven, my family had a picnic by a river. I remember, really clearly, my older brother slipped and fell in the water. I can still see his face!

Dr. Robert Hale: Mm-hm.

Maya: But my mom and my brother both say it was me. I fell in, and he pulled me out.

Dr. Robert Hale: Ha! That's a classic. Let me guess. Your family tells this story a lot, at dinners and holidays?

Maya: Yes! How did you know? My uncle tells it every New Year. And it's so vivid! I can see the water, his wet shirt, everything.

Dr. Robert Hale: I believe you. Every time the story is told, you build it again, and it can feel more vivid. But vivid doesn't mean correct. In a famous study, students wrote down, the day after the Challenger space shuttle accident, how they heard the news. A few years later, many told a different story, and they were very sure. Vivid, and still wrong.

Maya: So… my brother might be right?

Dr. Robert Hale: He might be. Without a video, nobody can be sure.

Maya: So how does something become a memory in the first place?

Dr. Robert Hale: There are two steps. First, you notice something. But most of what we notice is transient. It lasts a few minutes, and then it's gone. The second step is what we call consolidation.

Maya: Consolidation. Okay, what does that mean in simple words?

Dr. Robert Hale: It's the process of making a memory stable, so it lasts. In other words, it's like pressing save on a document. And a lot of this happens while you sleep.

Maya: Wait, so sleep is actually part of studying?

Dr. Robert Hale: In a way. Sleep the night after learning is important. But the main point is this: you don't have to wait for the night. Even a short nap after class can enable your brain to retain what you studied. So when students say, "I studied all night!"… I try to stay polite.

Maya: Oh no. I was planning to do exactly that on Thursday night, before my statistics exam on Friday. So what's a better approach?

Dr. Robert Hale: You might pass on Friday. But how much will you remember a month later? That's the forgetting curve. After you learn something, you forget a lot very quickly, in the first day or two. Then forgetting slows down.

Maya: So the first day or two is the dangerous part.

Dr. Robert Hale: Dangerous, yes. But also useful. The best strategy I know is retrieval practice. It means pulling information out of your memory. For example, you close your notebook and write down everything you remember from class. Then you check. And here's the surprising part: it often works better if you wait until the next day, when you've forgotten a little. It feels harder, and that helps the memory last. Then do it again a few days later.

Maya: Not just reading my notes again?

Dr. Robert Hale: No, that's a common trap. Rereading feels easy, so you think you know it. But that feeling is transient too. Testing yourself feels harder, but that's where the benefit comes from. So here's my main point about rereading: do it out loud, not silently. Saying the words helps a little. It's not as strong as testing yourself, though.

Maya: Huh. That's a real insight for me, because I'm a big rereader. Can you sum it up for us?

Dr. Robert Hale: Sure. So to sum up: memory isn't a video camera. A memory can feel vivid and still be wrong. Sleep helps save what you learn. And testing yourself, over a few days, is one of the best ways to remember it.

Maya: You know, I came in here thinking my memory was a video camera. Now I think it's more like… a storyteller.

Dr. Robert Hale: Ha, I like that. That's exactly right.

Maya: Thank you, Dr. Hale. Um, I know who I'm calling after this. Not my brother. He'll just say, "I told you so." My mom has a box of old family photos. Maybe there's one from the river. Bye for now!
