Can a City Grow Without Getting Worse?

On the Campus Minds podcast, the host Maya interviews Dr. Grace Adeyemi, an urban planner, about what happens when cities grow.

1. Before you listen: key words

impact nouna strong effect
policy nounan official plan or set of rules made by a government or organization
density nounhow many people or things are in an area
infrastructure nounthe basic systems a city needs: roads, pipes, drains, power
sustainability nounusing resources in a way that can continue without harming the environment
constraint nouna limit on what you can do
participatory adjectiveinvolving the people who are affected
phenomenon nounsomething that happens or exists, especially something unusual
accommodate verbto provide space or housing for people
vulnerable (to) adjectiveeasily harmed or damaged

4. After you listen: discuss

  1. Think about a town or city you know well. Did it grow up, grow out, or both? What changed for the people who live there?

  3. In your culture or community, who usually makes decisions about new buildings, parks, or roads? Should residents have more say?

Transcript

Maya: Welcome back to Campus Minds! I'm Maya. Today's big question: can a city grow without getting worse? My guest is Dr. Grace Adeyemi. Welcome!

Dr. Grace Adeyemi: Glad to be here. I'm an urban planner. That means I help cities decide how to use their land. You know, where the homes, shops, and roads go.

Maya: Okay. I should warn you, I'm a bit of a skeptic today. I mean, I have my doubts. Every time my hometown grew, things got worse. More traffic, more noise, fewer trees.

Dr. Grace Adeyemi: Ha, you're not alone. My purpose today is to show that growth doesn't have to mean worse. A city can grow and get better, if it grows in the right way.

Maya: Mm-hm. So what's the wrong way?

Dr. Grace Adeyemi: Well, the most common problem is what planners call urban sprawl. In other words, the city spreads out onto farms and forests. So people have to drive everywhere.

Maya: Hmm. My hometown can't really spread out. It sits between two hills.

Dr. Grace Adeyemi: And sprawl has a big impact on money, too. The reason is that every new street needs infrastructure. I mean roads, water pipes, drains, power lines. Someone has to build all that, and fix it for years.

Maya: Hmm. But couldn't the city just build bigger roads? Then the traffic goes away, right?

Dr. Grace Adeyemi: You'd think so! But there's a well-known phenomenon called induced demand. What that means is, when you add more lanes, driving gets easier for a while. So more people drive. And after a few years, the traffic is often back.

Maya: Wait, so the bigger road makes more traffic? That explains Main Street back home. They widened it twice!

Dr. Grace Adeyemi: Often, yes. Not always. In my first job, the city added two lanes to a highway. Three years later, it was just as slow.

Maya: Right. So what's the answer?

Dr. Grace Adeyemi: My main point is this: cities should grow up, not out. We build more homes closer together, near buses and trains. Planners call this increasing density. Simply put, more people living in the same area. Sometimes that means changing city policy to allow it.

Maya: Hold on. Won't tall buildings make neighborhoods crowded? I don't want to live next to a giant tower.

Dr. Grace Adeyemi: That's a fair question. But density doesn't have to mean giant towers. Let me give you an example. In one small city I worked with, there was an empty parking lot next to a bus stop. We replaced it with four-story buildings. A bakery and a small clinic on the ground floor, apartments above, and a small sports field.

Maya: Wait, just four stories?

Dr. Grace Adeyemi: Yep, just four. Now people there can walk to buy bread or see a doctor. Many don't need a car every day. That's good for sustainability, because fewer car trips mean less fuel and cleaner air.

Maya: Okay, that sounds nicer than I expected.

Dr. Grace Adeyemi: Ha, people always picture the worst.

Maya: And you mentioned a second idea. Something about, um, residents?

Dr. Grace Adeyemi: Yes. The second part of my argument is about people. I believe in participatory planning. It just means the people who live in a place help plan it.

Maya: Mm. That sounds nice, but... do residents really know more than the experts?

Dr. Grace Adeyemi: Sometimes, yes. Not about everything, but about things that happen there every year. For instance, in that same project, our first plan put some homes in the lowest corner. Then an older man at a meeting said, "That corner floods every rainy season." None of our maps showed it!

Maya: Oh, wow.

Dr. Grace Adeyemi: So we moved the homes to higher ground and put the sports field in that low corner. Grass can handle a little water. Those families would have been very vulnerable to floods.

Maya: Nice story. But let me push back a little. Meetings take time. What if residents just say no to everything?

Dr. Grace Adeyemi: That's a real risk. Every project has constraints, like money, time, and space. And the city still has to accommodate new families somewhere. So participation doesn't mean residents can block everything. They help shape the plan, not stop it.

Maya: Hmm. Help shape it, not stop it. I like that, but I think it's harder in real life.

Dr. Grace Adeyemi: It is harder, I agree. But in my experience, it saves time. When residents help early, there are fewer fights later, so building often finishes sooner.

Maya: Before we finish, what's the big idea?

Dr. Grace Adeyemi: Well, it's really two ideas. First, grow up, not out: build homes closer together, near shops and buses. And second, bring residents into the planning. Do both, and a city can grow without getting worse.

Maya: Okay, I'll say this. The four-story buildings? You've convinced me. The meetings... ask me again in a year.

Dr. Grace Adeyemi: Ha! Fair enough. Come to one meeting with me, then we'll talk.

Maya: Deal! Thank you, Dr. Adeyemi. Thanks for listening! Take care!
